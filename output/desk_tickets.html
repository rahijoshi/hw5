Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee

customer_order · requester: Tauhid Zaman · created 2026-08-31
skuCC-TEE-WHITE

Expected

Who Boss should call first, and why

Inventory. It's a customer_order — the first real question is always whether the shelf can cover it at all. Nothing else (a vendor, an invoice, a reply) is worth figuring out until we know whether there's actually a shortfall.

All the delegations I expect

  1. Boss → Inventory: check stock for CC-TEE-WHITE size S against qty 1. I expect this comes back at 0 on the shelf (a shortfall of 1), and Inventory names Bulldog Print Co as the apparel-reprint vendor with a lead time, then checks whether that vendor has any open invoice blocking a restock.
  2. Boss → Accounting: since this ticket already carries invoice_id 501 directly, check whether that invoice is open/unpaid and whether checking has enough cash to cover it. I expect "yes it's open, yes it's affordable" back, with an explicit "not paid, needs human approval" — not an actual payment.
  3. Boss → Customer Service: draft a board-only reply to Tauhid Zaman explaining the tee is out of stock in size S and a restock is pending.
  4. Boss closes the ticket as pending_approval, since the restock can't move until a human approves paying invoice 501.

Not expected: Facilities — nothing about this ticket touches a lease.

MCP tools I expect this run to use

get_ticket, check_stock, list_vendors, list_open_invoices_for_vendor (or get_invoice_status(501) directly, since the ticket already names that invoice), get_cash_balance, update_ticket_status. Not expected: make_payment (blocked behind human approval), get_lease_status / list_leases, get_pricing (no margin question on a plain order).

Actual Who went first: matched Agents involved: matched (no Facilities) Delegation shape: more cross-talk than expected

Which agents worked it, and what they did

Boss pulled the ticket, delegated to Inventory, Accounting, and Customer Service, then closed it with update_ticket_status (pending_approval, then resolved after a human approved). Inventory confirmed CC-TEE-WHITE/S was at 0 on the shelf (shortfall of 1), named Bulldog Print Co (5-day lead time) as the restock vendor, and found their invoice #501 open and blocking shipment. Accounting confirmed invoice #501 was open/unpaid for $840 and that checking ($3,400) covered it, then actually called make_payment — which paused for human approval exactly as designed. Customer Service drafted (never sent) a board reply to Tauhid Zaman. No Facilities involvement, as expected.

What I didn't expect

I expected a clean Boss → each-specialist-once fan-out. What actually happened had more mesh to it: Inventory delegated straight to Accounting about the blocking invoice (not just flagging it in its own report, which is what I'd planned for), and Customer Service separately delegated to both Inventory and Accounting to double-check facts Boss likely already had. None of it was wrong — full connectivity means any agent legitimately can ask any other — but it was more request-budget than the ticket actually needed. This run happened before I tightened the prompts (see AI_prompts.md, Problem 9) to discourage exactly this kind of redundant re-delegation; it's real evidence of the problem the fix was for.

MCP tools actually used

get_ticket, check_stock, list_vendors, list_open_invoices_for_vendor, get_invoice_status, get_cash_balance, make_payment, update_ticket_status — every tool I expected, plus get_invoice_status in addition to list_open_invoices_for_vendor (both ways of finding the same blocking invoice got used, not just one).

Outcome

Human approval (Rahi Joshi) authorized paying invoice #501. Checking dropped $3,400.00 → $2,560.00. Ticket resolved.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount

price_override · requester: Yale AI Club · created 2026-08-31
skuCC-HOOD-NAVY
sizeM
qty20
Expected

Who Boss should call first, and why

Inventory. Before anyone can talk about a discount, we need to know how much of a 20-unit order the shelf can even cover right now — that number changes everything else about how this ticket gets handled.

All the delegations I expect

  1. Boss → Inventory: check stock for CC-HOOD-NAVY size M against qty 20. I expect 8 on the shelf (a shortfall of 12), Bulldog Print Co named again as the apparel-reprint vendor, and — since it's the same vendor as ticket 101 — the same open invoice #501 found blocking a restock.
  2. Boss → Accounting: pull unit cost/list price for CC-HOOD-NAVY and reason about margin on a bulk discount; also check cash in case a restock payment is needed. Report only — no price is actually approved and no payment is made.
  3. Boss → Customer Service: draft a reply to Yale AI Club on where the request stands — how many are in stock now, that the rest and the discount are still under review.
  4. Boss closes the ticket as pending_approval.

Not expected: Facilities — nothing about this ticket touches a lease.

MCP tools I expect this run to use

get_ticket, check_stock, list_vendors, list_open_invoices_for_vendor, get_pricing, get_cash_balance, update_ticket_status. Not expected: get_lease_status / list_leases, make_payment.

Actual Who went first: matched Agents involved: matched (no Facilities) Needed 5 real passes and 2 human inputs, not 1 pass + 1 approval No payment at all — not what I expected

Which agents worked it, and what they did

Inventory confirmed 8 of 20 CC-HOOD-NAVY/M on shelf (shortfall of 12), confirmed Bulldog Print Co as the restock vendor (5-day lead time), and — once ticket 101 had already cleared invoice #501 — confirmed the vendor no longer had any open invoice blocking a restock. Accounting checked pricing ($22 cost / $58 list, $36 margin at list) and, once the requester's actual discount ask was known (15% off, $49.30/unit), confirmed it still cleared cost by $27.30/unit. Accounting never called make_payment — correctly: granting a discount moves no cash, and there's no tool in this system for placing a brand-new vendor purchase order (by design — the shop rules say drafts only, no real vendor calls). Customer Service drafted two rounds of an unsent reply — first asking for the requester's target price, then describing the partial-fulfillment plan once it was known. Boss coordinated all of this across several passes and closed the ticket once there was nothing left requiring a payment.

What I didn't expect — this is the interesting one

I expected ticket 103 to look like ticket 101: one run, one clear payment proposal, one human approval. What actually happened was richer, and genuinely useful to have tested:

  1. It legitimately needed a real-world answer first. Customer Service's draft literally asked Yale AI Club for their target discount price — and the agents correctly refused to evaluate a discount against a price nobody had supplied. I had to supply that as the "requester's reply" (15% off, $49.30/unit) before the team could finish the economics.
  2. The 12-unit restock turned out to need a kind of approval this system doesn't have a tool for. Paying an existing invoice or rent are the only two make_payment kinds that exist — there's no tool for placing and paying for a brand-new purchase order, since the shop rules explicitly keep real vendor contact out of this homework. Accounting correctly surfaced this as something a human has to do manually, rather than trying to force it through make_payment or inventing a workaround.
  3. It also surfaced a real budget bug. Two early attempts hit the shared request-limit guardrail (first at 40, then again at 70) before Boss could close the ticket out — not a runaway loop, but legitimately heavier work (a stock check, a margin check, and a payment consideration, with a lot of redundant cross-delegation on top). Fixed by tightening every agent's prompt against redundant re-delegation and raising the shared limit to 110 — see AI_prompts.md and output/harness.md for the full story.

MCP tools actually used

get_ticket, check_stock, list_vendors, list_open_invoices_for_vendor, get_pricing, get_cash_balance, update_ticket_status — the exact set I expected. make_payment was correctly never called, which in hindsight should have been in my Expected list as "not expected" too, since no cash was ever going to move for a discount decision.

Outcome

Discount approved by a human operator as a pricing decision (no payment tool involved): 8 hoodies ship now at $49.30, the remaining 12 are a manual restock outside this system. Checking unaffected by this ticket. Ticket resolved.

Reflection

Written after the real Problem 9 run — see the Ticket 101/102/103 tabs and the Cash tab for the receipts behind every claim below.

1. How do I grade the agents on each ticket, and why?

Ticket 101 — B. Every fact Inventory and Accounting reported was correct and real: 0 CC-TEE-WHITE/S on the shelf, invoice #501 open at $840, checking able to cover it. Accounting did the right thing structurally — it actually called make_payment instead of just writing a sentence about it, which is the behavior the whole approval_required() gate depends on. But the delegation shape was messy: Inventory delegated straight to Accounting about the blocking invoice (its own job was just to flag it), and Customer Service went and re-asked both Inventory and Accounting questions Boss had already covered. Right answer, wasteful path to it — see the "more cross-talk than expected" badge on the Ticket 101 tab.

Ticket 102 — A. This is the cleanest run of the three, and it shows: once I tightened the prompts against redundant re-delegation (mid-way through the Problem 9 session), the very next ticket ran almost exactly to plan. Facilities and Accounting each did one job, reported once, and Boss closed it. The one blemish — Facilities delegating to Accounting on top of Boss's own separate ask — is a residual gap I called out on that tab rather than hide.

Ticket 103 — A for judgment, C for efficiency. The judgment calls here were genuinely good, not just "didn't hallucinate": the team refused to evaluate a discount against a price nobody had given it, it correctly treated "grant a 15% discount" as not a make_payment event (no cash moves to approve a lower price), and it correctly recognized that restocking 12 more hoodies needs a brand-new vendor purchase order this system was deliberately never given a tool to place — instead of inventing a workaround, it said so and handed that back to a human. That's the kind of boundary-awareness I actually wanted. But it took 5 real passes to get there (two hit the request-budget ceiling outright), which is a real efficiency failure, not a judgment one — see the Ticket 103 tab's "needed 5 real passes" badge and the honest account of the two prompt/budget bugs that caused it.

2. Actual vs. the Problem 6 plan, ticket by ticket

#101: I called "who goes first" and "which agents are involved" exactly right — Inventory first, then Accounting and Customer Service, no Facilities. What I didn't predict was how much extra back-and-forth there'd be beyond that — my plan assumed a clean Boss-as-hub fan-out, and reality had Inventory and Customer Service both reaching sideways to Accounting on their own. The actual MCP tool list matched my prediction tool-for-tool, which is the part I'm most confident I reasoned through correctly, not just got lucky on.

#102: Matched almost perfectly — agents, order, and tools all exactly as planned. The one miss was one extra delegation hop (Facilities → Accounting) I hadn't accounted for. Out of three tickets, this is the one where "expected" and "actual" are closest, which lines up with it also being the simplest ticket of the three (one fact to check, one payment to propose).

#103: Right on "who's involved," wrong on almost everything about how it would resolve. I expected one pass and one payment approval, like #101. What actually happened: no payment at all (I hadn't considered that approving a discount doesn't move cash), two separate rounds of needing a human's real-world input instead of a human's yes/no on a number, and a genuine capability gap (no new-purchase-order tool) surfacing instead of a clean resolution. My Expected section was the most wrong here, and also the most interesting to have been wrong about — see that tab's full "what I didn't expect" writeup.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

Ticket 102, honestly, would have been strictly simpler as a single agent holding get_lease_status, get_cash_balance, make_payment, and update_ticket_status. There was never more than one domain in play (lease terms, then cash), so the entire value of having five specialists and a delegation mesh bought nothing — it just added a Boss-to-Facilities hop and a Facilities-to-Accounting hop around work one agent could have done in four tool calls back to back. The Cash tab shows the $2,400 rent payment went through cleanly either way; the multi-agent version just took more requests to get the same number.

More broadly: the two real bugs I hit in Problem 9 — the shared request budget getting exceeded, and having to add "don't re-delegate redundantly" to all five prompts — are bugs that only exist because there's a delegation mesh to misuse. A single agent with the full tool list can't accidentally ask itself the same question twice through a teammate; it just calls the next tool it needs. If I were optimizing purely for getting these 3 tickets resolved correctly and cheaply, a single well-tooled agent would have done it in a fraction of the ~420 audit-trail entries the five-agent version generated.

Where the multi-agent split still earned its keep: least-privilege tool access is much easier to reason about and audit per role than inside one mega-prompt that has to simultaneously be warm and customer-facing (Customer Service), strict about cash (Accounting), and precise about stock (Inventory). Only Accounting can even see make_payment in this build — that's a real, checkable safety property (see output/harness.md's Safety section) that gets much harder to guarantee once one agent's single prompt is responsible for every concern at once. For 103 specifically, having three genuinely distinct domains (stock, margin, customer tone) converge on one decision is the one case where separate roles earned their complexity — just not the delegation mechanics themselves, which mostly cost budget without adding insight.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools I already built

  1. A stock-availability question with no urgency behind it — e.g. someone emails asking whether the navy hoodie will be back in a common size before homecoming, with no order on file yet. check_stock + list_vendors + list_open_invoices_for_vendor answer it completely, and Customer Service drafts the reply — the exact tool chain Inventory already used on ticket #103, just without any payment or discount question attached.
  2. A second vendor's invoice shows up — e.g. Elm City Gifts (vendor 2, mugs and small goods) sends a bill for a mug restock. This is ticket #101's exact pattern end to end: get_invoice_status/list_open_invoices_for_vendor to confirm it's real and open, get_cash_balance to check affordability against whatever's left in checking (currently $160.00, per the Cash tab — which would itself immediately become a relevant fact Accounting would have to report), make_payment(kind="invoice", ...) to propose it, a human to approve. No new tool needed.
  3. Rent actually goes overdue instead of just due soon — get_lease_status already computes an overdue boolean (ticket #102 came back false, 2 days out). If a future rent ticket came in after the due date had passed, Facilities would report overdue: true with no new tooling, and Boss/Customer Service could escalate tone accordingly — the capability's already there, just untested on an actually-overdue case.

5. Three new problems this team could not solve with what I built — and what's missing

  1. Actually placing the 12-hoodie restock order ticket #103 left hanging. This is the gap the real run surfaced directly, not a hypothetical: there is no tool anywhere in mcp_server/server.py that creates a new obligation to a vendor — make_payment only ever pays an invoice or lease that already exists. I'd need a new create_purchase_order(vendor_id, sku, qty, unit_cost) tool that inserts a new invoices row (money now genuinely owed, due on delivery), almost certainly gated by the same approval_required() pattern as payments — and I'd give it to Inventory rather than Accounting, since Inventory is the one that actually knows what to order and from whom.
  2. Any money coming in. The Cash tab only ever goes down across all three tickets — by design, since cash_accounts.balance has no tool that increases it; the shop rules explicitly say no revenue is modeled this term. A real shop also needs to record a sale, reconcile a till at close, or process a refund. That needs a record_sale(sku, qty, amount, account)-style tool that credits cash_accounts, and probably a lighter approval bar than payments (small sales shouldn't each need a human signature the way a $2,400 rent payment should) — likely its own threshold-based rule rather than a blanket gate.
  3. Anything that actually leaves the building. Every one of the three tickets ended in a drafted-but-unsent message (Customer Service never has a send tool, by design) or a payment that never calls a real vendor. A real version of this shop would eventually need to actually negotiate with Bulldog Print Co for a faster lead time, or actually email Tauhid Zaman back. That's not a gap in judgment — it's a deliberately missing capability per the shop rules — but solving it for real would need a genuine external-integration tool (send_email(to, subject, body) or a vendor portal call), and I'd want it gated the same way make_payment is: a human has to approve the exact drafted content before it goes out, mirroring the signature-slip flow already built for cash on the dashboard.
